# Break 002 — the journal: `renamed` leaves the vocabulary, `buried` takes its place

**Date:** 2026-08-19 · **Format:** `journal` · **Epoch:** 1 (unchanged)

> The second note, and the first one that touches the **truth layer**. Break 001
> changed a derived artifact; this one changes the vocabulary of the records
> everything else is projected from, which is the heavier of the two by
> construction — a catalog can be rebuilt from the journal, and the journal
> cannot be rebuilt from anything.

## What changed on the wire

The `renamed` arm is gone. Before:

```json
{"kind":"renamed","from":["org.vibevm","wal-old"],"to":["org.vibevm.core","wal"]}
```

A new arm stands in its place — not as a translation of it, but as the one
**retirement** fact, of which a rename is a special case:

```json
{"kind":"buried","name":"wal-old","reason":"renamed to `wal`","superseded_by":"org.vibevm.core/wal"}
```

`superseded_by` is optional on both sides and skipped when absent, so a name
closed with nowhere to go is three keys rather than four. No other arm changes,
and no record already written is affected — see *Who fixes it*.

## Why the two collapse into one

The standing naming law says a **rename is a new identity**: versions never
transfer, and no coordinate is reused for different content. A first-class
rename relation would assert continuity between the old coordinate and the new
one — exactly what that law forbids. «The old name is closed, and here is where
to look instead» is the only model consistent with it, and that model is a
tombstone, which this format already had.

The deciding detail is smaller and harder to argue with: `renamed` carried
`from` and `to` and **no reason**, while a tombstone cannot exist without one.
Keeping both arms would have forced a choice between synthesising prose into a
required field and adding a reason to `renamed` — after which the two differed
only in whether the successor was optional, which is one thing spelled twice.

The reverse direction is not lost, only relocated: a reader holding the NEW name
learns the old one from the journal, which keeps every retirement fact forever.
That is the right home — the catalog answers *where do I go*, the journal
answers *what happened*.

## What it buys beyond tidiness

The projector gains its **first producing arm**. Until now every arm either
folded a version into the catalog or refused by name; the tombstone carrier was
filled only by *reading* a catalog off disk, and the projection built from the
journal never set it. Since the journal phase there is no read-then-write path —
a mutation builds its state from the fold and writes that out — so a tombstone
placed by anything other than a fact would have been erased by the next
unrelated publish, silently, with nothing going red.

Nothing could reach that before, because nothing produced a tombstone at all.
This change makes the producer a fact rather than a field write, which is what
closes the hole rather than merely not opening it. The guard is a test that
fails on the field-writing implementation and passes on this one
(`journal/burial_tests.rs::a_tombstone_survives_an_unrelated_publish`, proved
red by neutering the producer before it was believed green).

## Why it is a break rather than an addition

A reader of the old vocabulary meeting `buried` refuses it by tag — the union is
tagged and an unknown `kind` is rejected, not guessed at. That refusal is the
point: it announces itself. And a reader of the new vocabulary meeting a
`renamed` record would refuse it the same way, which is why the absence of any
such record on disk is what makes the change free rather than merely cheap.

## Why no epoch

Same reasoning as Break 001, and one measurement of its own. An epoch mints a
parallel world and costs a support window; this change needs neither. There are
zero external consumers of the journal (the generated type is used by exactly
one thing in this repository — the differential wire-parity oracle — and by
nothing outside it), the system is pre-publication (`public = false`, migrations
do not run), and — the fact that decides it — **no record anywhere carries
`"kind":"renamed"` as data.** That was measured rather than assumed, beside a
control proving the instrument does find records: the same search finds
`"kind":"yanked"` in `formats/corpora/index/e1/state/journal/2026-08.ndjson`.

There is therefore no old world to run in parallel: the arm existed in the
vocabulary and was never once exercised.

## Who fixes it

Nobody downstream, and nobody upstream either. No journal on disk holds a
`renamed` record — not the golden corpus, not a fixture, not a test's committed
bytes. The only sites that ever constructed one were three test fixtures and the
projector's own refusal branch, all of which move with this change.

An operator holding a journal written by an older build is unaffected unless
that journal contains a rename, and none can.

## Sunset

None. The old arm is not published in parallel and is not supported after this
change. A reader that must understand both worlds does not exist and is not
planned.

## User recipe

None. There is nothing to migrate: no stored record changes shape, and the
catalog projected from any existing journal is byte-identical before and after
(`cargo xtask rebuild --check formats/corpora/index/e1` proves it on the golden
corpus). An operator who wants the new verb waits for the command that emits the
fact; the fact and its projection land first, deliberately, so the verb has
somewhere to write.

## Notes for the next note

Written while `public = false`, where a note is still optional — kept anyway for
the reason Break 001 gives, and for one this note adds: **the cheapness of a
truth-layer break is a property of a moment, not of the layer.** Every argument
above leans on «nothing has recorded one yet», and that sentence stops being
true the first time an operator buries a name. A vocabulary change that is free
today is a migration tomorrow, and the note is where that distinction is
recorded rather than remembered.
